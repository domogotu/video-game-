# Living Creature Ecosystem and Collection-Inspired Discovery

**Project:** Lethal Absorption  
**Status:** Design specification — proposed, not implemented or tested  
**Purpose:** Add a broad, discoverable creature ecosystem inspired by the appeal of creature-collecting games: varied species, habitats, rarity, behaviors, bonds, research, team/squad choices where appropriate, and surprising encounters. All creatures, designs, names, lore, and mechanics must be original to Lethal Absorption rather than copied from Pokémon or another franchise.

## 1. Core fantasy

The world should feel inhabited by many distinct lifeforms that players can encounter, observe, learn about, interact with, befriend, compete against, avoid, hunt when appropriate, or study for evolutionary possibilities. Finding an unusual creature should feel exciting even when the player does not absorb or defeat it.

This ecosystem complements the central fantasy of **Consume. Evolve. Survive.** It does not replace absorption, body evolution, or action combat with a separate creature-collection game.

## 2. Creature identity record

Each species should have a data record containing:
- Unique original name, silhouette, visual identifiers, animation language, and sound profile.
- Species family and ecological role (producer, grazer, scavenger, predator, symbiont, parasite, decomposer, social guardian, migratory species, or other role).
- Preferred habitat and the conditions it can tolerate.
- Diet, activity cycle, movement style, sensory strengths, and social behavior.
- Threat level, defensive behavior, escape behavior, and combat telegraphs if hostile.
- Elemental, material, biological, or environmental affinities where useful.
- Potential interactions: observe, track, feed, evade, assist, bond, defend, battle, research, or absorb, depending on species and context.
- Discovery clues, compatible absorption outcomes, and possible evolutionary relevance.
- Rarity band, population rules, breeding/reproduction rules if appropriate, and conservation/ecosystem effects.
- Whether the species is sapient, culturally significant, protected, or unsuitable for absorption.

Not every species needs every field or every interaction. The record must explicitly state what applies.

## 3. Habitats and ecosystem relationships

Creatures should appear because the environment supports them, not only because a random spawn roll selected them. Habitats can include:
- Cell-scale nutrient fields, microbial colonies, and microscopic predators.
- Grasslands, forests, treetops, deserts, wetlands, rivers, lakes, coastlines, reefs, and deep water.
- Mountains, cliffs, caves, underground networks, ruins, settlements, and enemy dens.
- Alien biomes, extreme-weather regions, orbital habitats, and living-ship/cosmic environments in later stages.

Each habitat defines climate and environmental states, resources, cover, travel routes, shelter, dangers, and species interactions. Day/night, season, weather, migration, and player-caused disturbances may alter which species are present. Large-scale simulation must be budgeted so distant ecosystems can use lower-cost abstract simulation.

## 4. Creature roles and behavior

Species should have recognizable behaviors rather than only different statistics:
- **Shy:** flee, hide, or freeze when approached.
- **Curious:** approach unfamiliar objects or investigate player traces.
- **Territorial:** warn first, then defend a defined area.
- **Pack/social:** coordinate, communicate, protect young, or assist allies.
- **Ambush predator:** use cover and readable warning signs.
- **Scavenger:** gather remains or exploit disturbances.
- **Migratory:** move between habitat zones as conditions change.
- **Symbiotic:** provide a benefit when a compatible player or species helps them.
- **Rare wanderer:** appear under specific ecological conditions or during unusual events.
- **Sapient:** make choices according to culture, goals, relationships, and agency—not a simple collectible state.

Behaviors need readable tells, consistent counterplay, and recovery states. A species should not switch arbitrarily between friendly and hostile without a cause the player can learn.

## 5. Encounter, collection, and interaction loop

A typical encounter can follow this loop:

1. Notice tracks, calls, environmental signs, rumors, or a direct sighting.
2. Observe the creature and learn about its habitat, temperament, strengths, and needs.
3. Choose how to approach: avoid it, sneak past, help it, feed it, defend against it, battle it, research it, form a bond, or absorb it when appropriate.
4. Resolve the interaction through the relevant gameplay system.
5. Add a record to the Discovery Network, including known behaviors and verified facts.
6. Gain an outcome appropriate to the interaction: knowledge, trust, a resource, an alliance, an ability clue, an evolutionary possibility, or no immediate reward.
7. Let the ecosystem respond where the interaction has meaningful consequences.

**No single interaction is mandatory for every species.** Some creatures cannot be befriended; some are dangerous; some are better observed than fought; some are sapient and must be treated as agents rather than loot.

## 6. Bonding and companion possibilities

For compatible non-sapient species, a bond may develop through repeated non-coercive interactions, feeding, shared danger, care, or meeting species-specific needs. Bonding is not guaranteed and should depend on species behavior and the player's actions.

Possible companion roles include scouting, warning of danger, tracking, carrying small resources, helping in combat, or assisting with traversal. A companion must have strengths and limits, be vulnerable to understandable risks, and not solve every encounter automatically.

Companions are optional. Players who prefer solitary play must be able to progress. If companions can be lost, the game must clearly communicate risk and avoid arbitrary permanent loss from invisible systems. Companion rules for sapient allies are distinct from animal bonding.

## 7. Collection journal and discovery network

The player’s journal records:
- Seen, observed, studied, bonded, battled, absorbed, and fully understood states separately.
- Confirmed facts versus hypotheses and rumors.
- Habitat, activity patterns, diet, defenses, and environmental sensitivities.
- Previously seen variants and unusual appearances.
- Known compatible absorption clues, not guaranteed trait drops.
- Ecosystem effects caused by major player actions.
- Optional research goals that reward observation, tracking, protection, or experimentation—not only combat.

Sharing a verified record through the Discovery Network shares knowledge, not automatic ownership, mastery, or another character’s bond. Online sharing, if implemented later, must be designed without requiring multiplayer for core completion.

## 8. Variants, rarity, luck, and discovery

Use rarity bands such as Common, Uncommon, Rare, Very Rare, and Exceptional. Rarity may result from population scarcity, habitat requirements, migration, time or weather conditions, unusual traits, or a limited random component.

- A rare encounter should have clues or ecological logic when practical, so players can learn how to seek it.
- Random luck can produce an early encounter or unusual variant, consistent with the Luck and Random Discovery System specification.
- Do not make essential progression depend on finding a particular random creature.
- Provide alternate research or encounter paths after repeated failure or prolonged absence.
- Variants should have readable differences and a reason to exist; avoid purely cosmetic clutter that overwhelms the journal.
- Shiny-style rarity can be an optional inspiration at the broad design level, but all visual rules, terminology, odds, and presentation must be original. Do not reproduce Pokémon's specific shiny system, designs, names, or presentation.
- Rare does not automatically mean stronger. Some rare species offer unique utility, knowledge, or ecology rather than raw power.

## 9. Absorption and ethical/ecological consequences

Absorption remains a core player power, but it must follow compatibility and world rules. An encounter can yield biomass, resources, clues, partial trait information, a possible mutation, or no new power. It must not automatically grant every characteristic of the target.

- Absorbing common organisms may alter local resource availability if the scale and simulation budget make that meaningful.
- Removing keystone species can affect food chains, migration, pests, predators, or habitat stability.
- The player can choose observation, avoidance, aid, bonding, or research instead of absorption where supported.
- Sapient beings and culturally significant species are not ordinary resource containers. Their rights, consent, culture, and story consequences must be treated separately and seriously.
- Do not punish ordinary experimentation with invisible catastrophic ecosystem collapse. Consequences should be proportionate, signaled, and recoverable when appropriate.
- The game must not require cruelty to complete a core path.

## 10. Combat and player growth

Creature encounters should teach pattern recognition and adaptation:
- Different body plans and defenses require different tactics.
- Environmental advantages can matter more than raw level.
- Players can learn a species' tells, resistances, vulnerabilities, escape routes, and social behavior.
- A creature's level or rarity must not make it immune to player agency; strong opponents need readable mechanics and counterplay.
- Creature-versus-creature interactions should be simulated only at a detail level appropriate to distance and performance.
- No single type chart or elemental system is required. If affinities are used, their interactions must be consistent, explainable, and open to discovery.

## 11. Stage-specific use

- **Cellular opening:** only a few legible organisms and nutrient sources. Teach observation and absorption without introducing a giant encyclopedia.
- **Animal-scale exploration:** make habitat, tracks, cover, food, threats, and simple species behaviors central.
- **Apex creature stage:** introduce territory, dens, rival predators, symbiosis, specialized prey, and more consequential ecosystem choices.
- **Main-form adventure:** creatures support traversal, combat, puzzles, allies, research, settlements, and regional stories.
- **Living Ship/cosmic stage:** discover alien biospheres, space-dwelling organisms, planetary life signatures, and rare cosmic species; return to an appropriate surface body for ground-level interactions.

Introduce the journal and deeper collection mechanics gradually. Do not overwhelm the first 5–10 minutes with a full species catalogue.

## 12. Balance, performance, and validation

When implemented, verify:
- Species spawn only in valid habitats and conditions.
- Behavioral states have valid transitions and readable telegraphs.
- Rare encounters are uncommon but not effectively impossible.
- Required progression remains possible without rare spawns.
- Repeatedly removing a species has only the intended, tested ecosystem impact.
- Companion behavior does not block traversal or trivialize combat.
- Discovery records distinguish rumor, observation, confirmed research, absorption, and mastery.
- Random outcomes are reproducible for debugging and save/load integrity.
- Distant creatures use bounded low-cost simulation; nearby creatures receive full gameplay behavior.
- Species, variants, and behaviors are original and pass an IP review before release.

## 13. Scope boundary

This document defines the ecosystem design, not a completed creature roster or implemented system. The initial vertical slice should include only a small, curated set of organisms with distinct roles. Full roster size, exact rarity rates, bonding depth, companion commands, breeding, and ecosystem simulation complexity remain deferred until tested.

**Current state:** Specified at design level. Not implemented, playtested, or verified in a playable build.
