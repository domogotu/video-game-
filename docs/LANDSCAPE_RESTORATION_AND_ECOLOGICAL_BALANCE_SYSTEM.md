# Lethal Absorption — Landscape Restoration and Ecological Balance System

> **Status:** Design specification; not implemented, simulated, or tested.  
> **Scope:** Veyra and future planets, with planet-specific species, societies, methods, hazards, and recovery rates.  
> **Core principle:** Nature is maintained by competing processes. Restoration is active work, not an automatic visual reset.

## 1. Goals

- Selected species, colonies, and sapient communities repair landscapes over time.
- Restoration is visible: replanting, seed dispersal, soil rebuilding, erosion control, waterway recovery, pollination, decomposition, and habitat construction.
- Other species slow, redirect, or prevent recovery through feeding, trampling, burrowing, competition, disease, territorial behavior, and disturbance.
- Ecosystems and restoration practices can spread or be moved between regions when conditions and compatibility allow.
- Player actions have persistent consequences without making any species universally good or evil.

## 2. Landscape state and recovery

Each landscape patch tracks a state such as intact, disturbed, actively recovering, constrained, stable, degraded, or locally collapsed. State changes depend on conditions and events, not a universal timer.

Relevant variables include native plant cover and age, seed bank, soil stability and fertility, moisture, microbial activity, water quality and flow, deadwood and shelter, pollinator/disperser activity, herbivory, predation, disease, invasive pressure, repeated disturbance, habitat connectivity, restoration agents and their resources, and the cause/severity of damage.

Green cover does not mean a healthy ecosystem has returned. Grass and pioneer plants may recover before mature trees, soil structure, species diversity, and complex habitat. A patch can recover into a different but ecologically valid state, remain degraded, or collapse locally if pressure persists.

## 3. Restoration guilds

Species dossiers may assign one or more roles. These are functional guilds, not moral alignments.

### Seed and propagule dispersers
Carry viable seeds, spores, eggs, plant fragments, or beneficial microbes on the body, through digestion, or by deliberate caching. They connect separated habitat patches. Transport requires a viable route, propagule survival, and a suitable destination.

**Veyra candidate:** Moss-Horn Browser is already proposed as a seed disperser. The exact seeds, transport mechanism, and deposition conditions still need a documented design rule.

### Soil and substrate engineers
Turn soil, aerate sediment, mix organic matter, create burrows, expose substrate, and redistribute nutrients. These actions can help seedlings but may also damage roots, destabilize slopes, or drain wetlands.

**Veyra candidate:** Root-Crown Forager is proposed as a burrowing omnivore / soil engineer. Effects depend on density, soil moisture, and location.

### Planting and tending organisms
Cache viable seeds, bury them at suitable depth, clear limited competition, protect seedlings, or tend host plants. These behaviors cost time and energy and can be interrupted by threats or scarcity.

### Pollinators and reproductive partners
Move pollen or other reproductive material between compatible plants. A pollination benefit is credited only when transfer creates a measurable reproductive benefit.

### Waterway and wetland maintainers
Create channels, maintain pools, build dams or levees, filter water, or move wetland plant material. These actions may restore one area while flooding, drying, or blocking another. Water engineering redistributes water; it does not create unlimited water.

### Decomposers and nutrient recyclers
Break down deadwood, carcasses, litter, and waste into usable nutrients. They clear some debris but may have species-specific risks such as spore or disease transmission.

### Habitat builders and repairers
Build nests, reefs, living root mats, shelters, or protective barriers that support other species. Structures require materials and upkeep. Losing a builder population can cause delayed losses among dependent species.

### Sapient ecological stewards
People, clans, settlements, or alien societies may cultivate native plants, reforest slopes, restore wetlands, maintain migration corridors, remove contamination, raise suitable stock, or relocate organisms. These are autonomous agents with goals, culture, territory, knowledge, and resource limits—not tools that automatically obey the player. Cooperation may depend on trust, trade, negotiation, research, and protection. Their plans can fail through conflict, scarcity, poor knowledge, or unsuitable choices.

## 4. Counter-restoration traits

Some organisms naturally prevent, reverse, or redirect restoration. These are survival strategies, not arbitrary sabotage.

- **Seed predators** consume seeds before germination.
- **Heavy grazers and browsers** eat seedlings and young shoots. At moderate density they may limit competitive plants; at high density they may prevent tree recruitment.
- **Root or bark feeders** weaken selected plants.
- **Tramplers** crush seedlings, compact soil, or damage streambanks along regular routes.
- **Burrowers and excavators** improve soil in one patch but destabilize slopes or roots elsewhere.
- **Territorial suppressors** exclude dispersers, pollinators, or habitat builders.
- **Resource competitors** consume water, nesting material, nutrients, or host plants needed for recovery.
- **Pathogen, parasite, or blight carriers** affect compatible hosts only when a credible transmission route exists.
- **Disturbance-associated species** create or exploit fire, flood, or open ground, sometimes maintaining grassland while limiting woodland expansion.
- **Introduced colonizers** may outcompete native regeneration where the planet's rules support it; non-native does not automatically mean harmful.
- **Restoration hunters** prey on dispersers, pollinators, grazers, or habitat builders, changing recovery indirectly.

A trait can help or harm depending on density and habitat. Grazing may preserve open grassland but stop forest regeneration when excessive. Burrowing can aerate soil and undermine banks. Seed-eating can limit recruitment, while seed-caching by the same species may aid dispersal. Species should not be labeled villains: their behavior serves their own survival and reproduction.

## 5. Conditions, bottlenecks, and outcomes

Restoration requires suitable conditions:
- Seeds need viable source stock, compatible substrate, moisture/temperature, and a survival window.
- Saplings need space, nutrients, time, and protection from excessive browsing.
- Wetlands need a suitable water table and water quality.
- Pollination needs compatible reproductive stages and successful transfer.
- Soil recovery needs organic input, microbial activity, and tolerable erosion.
- Relocation needs viable organisms or propagules, destination suitability, and a safe transfer method.

Rates are constrained by food, water, energy, population size, season, and carrying capacity. If damage outpaces recovery, the patch stays degraded or changes into a different stable ecosystem.

Possible outcomes include resilient recovery, patchwork recovery, an alternative stable state, stalled recovery from missing seeds or excessive browsing, feedback collapse after a key species disappears, or managed coexistence supported by players or sapient stewards. No species repairs every kind of damage. Every restoration trait needs a target, conditions, rate, cost, side effects, range, and counterplay.

## 6. Moving ecosystems between areas

Ecological transfer is possible but is not instant biome copying. Distinguish:

1. **Natural dispersal:** migration through viable corridors.
2. **Assisted migration:** move seeds, spores, microbes, eggs, juveniles, or adults to a suitable patch.
3. **Habitat restoration:** improve conditions and let native/compatible species recolonize.
4. **Ecological introduction:** move a species beyond its known range after compatibility and risk review.
5. **Invasive spread:** a species establishes with unintended consequences.
6. **Refugium creation:** protect a viable population or seed bank during local collapse.

Before transfer, evaluate climate, water, substrate, food, predators, competitors, disease/parasite risk, reproduction, route, and effects on residents. Record source population and lineage, condition/quarantine where relevant, quantity, transfer method, destination, and outcome. A moved species may fail, remain confined, alter the food web, or displace residents; no outcome is guaranteed.

Cross-planet transfer requires stricter gates than regional transfer. Atmosphere, chemistry, gravity, pathogens, reproductive biology, or other planetary conditions may make transfer impossible. Do not make every species portable to every world. Players may move ecological functions in stages—for example, restore dispersal and soil first, then introduce plants after the patch becomes viable.

## 7. Player tools and discovery

Possible tools unlocked through anatomy, research, crafting, or relationships:
- Observe landscape condition and limiting factors.
- Track dispersers, grazers, burrowers, pollinators, and habitat builders.
- Find damaged patches, seed sources, migration routes, and vulnerable seedlings.
- Gather and transport propagules without exhausting source populations.
- Build seed shelters, barriers, water controls, or nursery sites when anatomy and materials permit.
- Protect restoration agents, reduce disturbance, or negotiate stewardship.
- Relocate species after researching compatibility and risk.
- Monitor results across seasons and generations.

Discovery is gradual. Tracks, seedlings, browse marks, dung, burrows, calls, pollen, and changing water/soil conditions provide evidence. The field guide distinguishes observed fact, plausible hypothesis, and confirmed mechanism.

## 8. Persistence and destruction-recovery timing

Landscape succession is separate from the existing proposed short repair loop for damaged objects and structures. The rule that investigation/repair begins after five in-world minutes and ordinary repairable objects normally recover within twenty in-world minutes must not regrow mature forests or restore complex ecosystems.

Use two connected systems:
- **Incident response:** NPCs begin investigation, cleanup, and urgent repairs after the established delay; simple objects and structures follow their own recovery rules.
- **Ecological succession:** plants, soil, water, populations, and habitat complexity recover on species- and biome-specific schedules, potentially across many in-world days, seasons, or generations.

A burned tree may be permanently lost while its species returns through seeds or new growth. A destroyed habitat may need active replanting and long succession. Story-critical access retains a coherent path forward without requiring every ecosystem to return to its former state.

## 9. Data contract

Every restoration or counter-restoration trait records:
- Stable trait ID and owning species, lineage, or culture ID.
- Role and target entities / compatible biome IDs.
- Trigger, activity window, range, rate, energy/resource cost, and cooldown.
- Preconditions and failure reasons.
- Positive, negative, and indirect ecological effects.
- Population-density response and saturation limits.
- Interactions with predators, competitors, disease, weather, seasons, and player actions.
- Transferability and cross-planet compatibility restrictions.
- Discovery clues, evidence confidence, simulation tier, persistence needs, and acceptance tests.
- Status: hypothesis, specified, implemented, tested, or rejected.

Each landscape patch links to disturbance cause, recovery stage, limiting factors, nearby restoration agents, seed/propagule sources, and habitat connectivity. Use stable IDs, not name-only links.

## 11. Acceptance tests required before implementation is called complete

- [ ] Damaged patches record cause, severity, recovery stage, and limiting factors.
- [ ] Dispersers move viable propagules only through valid routes into compatible destinations.
- [ ] Restoration consumes time/resources and cannot repair unlimited area instantly.
- [ ] Browsing, seed predation, trampling, or competition can slow recovery under defined conditions.
- [ ] The same species can help one habitat and harm another under consistent rules.
- [ ] Protecting or losing a restoration agent changes future habitat outcomes.
- [ ] Assisted migration checks compatibility, capacity, source-population impact, and transfer risk.
- [ ] Introduced species can fail, establish, or cause a documented food-web shift.
- [ ] Water engineering redistributes water without creating unlimited water.
- [ ] Ecological succession is not forced to finish within the short structure-repair timer.
- [ ] Aggregate simulation preserves meaningful trends and important populations.
- [ ] Multiplayer clients cannot duplicate restoration events, propagules, or population changes.
- [ ] UI distinguishes observations, hypotheses, specified mechanics, implementation, and tests.

**Evidence status:** Design-only. No landscape-restoration behavior, ecosystem transfer, or counter-restoration simulation is claimed implemented or tested.
