# Lethal Absorption — Species Dossier: Gloam Moth

> **Stable ID:** LA-PLN-INV-005  
> **Planet:** Veyra  
> **Status:** Designed — concept dossier; not implemented or tested  
> **Primary region:** Reedwater Basin's woodland edge, marsh margin, and seasonal flowering patches

## 1. Identity and ecological role

**Role:** Nocturnal flying invertebrate; nectar visitor and prey species.  
**Originality:** Original fictional organism; ecological effects are provisional.

The Gloam Moth is a soft-winged, dusk-active flier with broad wings and feathery sensory antennae. Adults visit night-opening flowers and feed on nectar or other energy-rich plant secretions. Larvae use **Reedlace Hostleaf (VEY-RES-PLT-006)** as the initial host candidate, consuming limited leaf tissue when seasonal leaf stock is available. This is a design hypothesis, not a validated biological interaction. Adults can transfer pollen between compatible flowers, but pollination effectiveness must be validated rather than assumed for every plant.

## 2. Anatomy and life stages

- **Body plan:** Two pairs of scaled wings, six legs, coiled or extendable feeding tube, compound eyes, and highly sensitive antennae.
- **Scale:** Small flying animal; exact wingspan, mass, and flight speed remain open.
- **Life stages:** Egg on a suitable host → feeding larva → pupal stage in sheltered vegetation or leaf litter → winged adult.
- **Reproduction:** Eggs are placed near a viable larval food source. Host availability, humidity, and temperature constrain successful development. Egg count and generation time remain undefined.
- **Diet:** Larvae consume specific plant/fungal resources; adults feed on nectar or comparable secretions.
- **Mortality:** Predation, host-plant loss, drought, cold snaps, artificial light disruption, and toxic contamination.

## 3. Habitat and distribution

**Habitat anchors:** Mossbank Edge, Elderbank Stand clearings, Longgrass Shelf flower patches, Reedglass Marsh margins, and seasonally wet Southwash Fan vegetation where host plants exist.

**Preferred conditions:** Connected flowering patches, larval host plants, sheltered pupation sites, and low enough disturbance to support breeding.  
**Poor conditions:** Hostless cleared ground, long droughts, cold periods outside tolerance, and intense artificial light that disrupts orientation or feeding.

Adults may disperse between nearby patches, but a gap's width, wind, and weather affect successful crossing. Seasonal emergence follows suitable temperature and food availability rather than a fixed universal calendar.

## 4. Senses and locomotion

- **Senses:** Compound vision, antennae that detect odor and airflow, and touch through feet and wing contact.
- **Movement:** Fluttering flight, short hovering approaches, landing, and sheltered rest.
- **Limits:** Wind and rain disrupt flight; flight consumes energy; dense vegetation blocks routes; wings can be damaged.
- **Player clues:** Wingbeat sound, drifting silhouettes at dusk, brief visits to flowers, and clusters around suitable host patches.
- **Fairness:** The moth's flight path is not perfectly predictable. Slime chemical sensing may detect nearby floral/animal traces, while vibration sensing alone is less useful for a flying target.

## 5. Behavior and AI contract

Minimum states: rest in cover → emerge when conditions are suitable → follow odor/visual cues to a resource patch → feed/visit flower → avoid immediate threat → return to cover or move to another patch.

- Weather, temperature, and time of day influence emergence and flight range.
- Repeated disturbance can shift visitation to nearby patches.
- Adults should not hover indefinitely; energy and weather eventually force rest.
- Larval survival depends on host availability and predation.
- Light attraction is conditional and can be disruptive; artificial lights do not teleport or endlessly spawn moths.

## 6. Ecology and proposed food-web links

- Adults may pollinate compatible night-opening flowers when their body and feeding behavior transfer pollen.
- Larvae consume Reedlace Hostleaf leaf tissue when available and can suppress plant growth when locally abundant; populations are constrained by host stock, season, weather, and predators.
- Adults and larvae may be prey for insectivores, including the Glass-Eyed Pouncer only if its size and capture mechanics support the interaction.
- The Bark Lantern is not automatically a predator of the moth; their overlap does not prove predation.
- Moth visitation can provide a readable clue to flowering patches, but not a universal indicator of plant safety or rarity.

Pollination and predator relationships remain hypotheses pending compatible plant and predator dossiers.

## 7. Resources and nonlethal study

- **Study:** Track emergence times, map flower visitation, identify larval host plants, and observe response to wind and light.
- **Nonlethal route:** Watch visitation patterns or scan a wing scale without capturing the animal.
- **Possible resource:** Wing scales or silk-like material are not confirmed resources; harvesting requires a justified mechanic and low-impact rules.
- **Harvest impact:** Collecting adults during breeding periods or removing host plants can collapse local recruitment.
- **Renewal:** Requires host plants, suitable pupation cover, seasonal weather, and connected flower patches.

## 8. Absorption and compatibility

A flying target creates a different challenge from ground prey: the Slime must predict landing points, exploit shelter or weather, and avoid wasting stamina on repeated failed grabs.

Potential learning branches:
- **Airflow Cue Reading:** Infer likely flight direction from local airflow and vegetation.
- **Landing-Window Timing:** Improve timing against brief grounded moments.
- **Pollen Trace Interpretation:** Detect recent flower visitation under appropriate sensory conditions.

Absorption does not automatically grant wings, sustained flight, compound vision, or unrestricted aerial movement. Any derived trait needs explicit anatomy, energy costs, progression and counters.

## 9. Skills and training

The Gloam Moth is not a trainer. Observation may contribute to:
- **Flight-Path Prediction:** Estimate short movement arcs from repeated observations.
- **Host-Plant Tracking:** Follow adult visitation to locate potential larval habitat.
- **Weather-Aware Movement:** Learn when wind or rain changes safe movement opportunities.

These are candidates until formal skill records define prerequisites, costs, practice, mastery, and counterplay.

## 10. Variants and discovery

Provisional regional forms:
- **Marsh Gloam Moth:** More frequent near damp margins and marsh flowers.
- **Elderwood Gloam Moth:** More likely around sheltered old-growth clearings and deep leaf litter.

They remain regional forms of one species. Discovery clues include dusk flight, flower visitation, host-plant damage, and pupation shelters.
