# Lethal Absorption — Species Dossier: Bark Lantern

> **Stable ID:** LA-PLN-INV-002  
> **Planet:** Veyra  
> **Status:** Designed — concept dossier; not implemented or tested  
> **Primary region:** Reedwater Basin woodland margins, Elderbank Stand, and humid understory

## 1. Identity and ecological role

**Role:** Nocturnal or crepuscular bark-dwelling invertebrate; small detritivore and occasional flower/lichen visitor.  
**Originality:** Original fictional organism. All biology and balance values are provisional.

The Bark Lantern is a small, six-limbed arthropod-like animal that shelters in bark fissures and hollow deadwood. A translucent abdominal plate diffuses a low-intensity bioluminescent glow. The glow is a communication and close-range signaling tool, not a flashlight that illuminates an entire forest. Individuals graze on soft fungal films, decomposing plant matter, and small surface organisms.

## 2. Anatomy and life stages

- **Body plan:** Six jointed limbs, segmented body, gripping tarsal pads, short antennae, and a light-diffusing abdominal organ.
- **Scale:** Small enough to hide in bark crevices; exact dimensions and mass remain open.
- **Life stages:** Egg cluster in protected damp crevice → soft-bodied juvenile → hardened subadult → reproductive adult → senescent adult.
- **Reproduction:** Eggs require humid shelter but not standing water. Desiccation and fungal imbalance can reduce survival; exact clutch size and development time are undefined.
- **Diet:** Fungal films, decaying plant material, and tiny bark-surface organisms.
- **Mortality:** Drying, cold snaps, predation, loss of deadwood, and contamination.

## 3. Habitat and distribution

**Habitat anchors:** Mossbank Edge, Elderbank Stand, shaded Longgrass Shelf margins, and humid woodland pockets around Quietroot Grotto.

**Preferred conditions:** Retained deadwood, rough bark, stable humidity, fungal growth, and connected shaded refuges.  
**Poor conditions:** Exposed sun-baked trunks, recently cleared woodland, chemically contaminated bark, and prolonged drought.

Seasonal rain increases surface activity; dry spells drive individuals deeper into crevices. Deadwood removal reduces shelter and food, so a cleared area should not repopulate instantly.

## 4. Senses and locomotion

- **Senses:** Antennae detect chemical cues and contact; leg joints register vibration; simple light-sensitive organs distinguish broad light/dark changes.
- **Movement:** Slow climbing on rough bark, short crossings between touching branches, and quick retreat into crevices.
- **Limits:** Smooth wet surfaces can be difficult; open ground exposes it to predators; it cannot fly.
- **Player clues:** Small glows in shaded bark, faint antenna movement, clustered specks near fungal films, and rapid dimming when disturbed.
- **Fairness:** Glow visibility varies with ambient light, distance, cover, and viewing angle. It does not reveal a precise outline through solid bark.

## 5. Behavior and AI contract

Minimum states: shelter → forage on surface → exchange brief light/chemical signals → detect disturbance → retreat into a crevice → resume activity after a cooldown.

- Individuals prefer existing cracks and hollows; they do not create large burrows.
- Repeated disturbance shifts activity deeper into shelter.
- Light signals can attract a mate or nearby conspecific, but do not summon a whole swarm from unlimited distance.
- Predators may learn the glow's association with prey; the species may reduce signaling when alarmed.
- Activity is reduced during extreme dryness and cold.

## 6. Ecology and proposed food-web links

- Processes fungal films and dead plant material, returning nutrients to the local bark/soil system.
- May be prey for Gloam Moth juveniles or other small insectivores only after their feeding mechanics are specified.
- May move small fungal propagules between nearby surfaces; this is a hypothesis, not a guaranteed global dispersal service.
- Deadwood retention increases habitat complexity; removing every fallen trunk should visibly reduce local abundance.
- It is not assumed to pollinate major flowering plants without evidence of flower visits and compatible anatomy.

All ecological links are provisional until the basin food-web review is validated.

## 7. Resources and nonlethal study

- **Study:** Observe signaling intervals, compare occupancy of damp and dry bark, and record retreat distances.
- **Nonlethal route:** Photograph/scan glow patterns or observe a crevice without removing the animal.
- **Possible resource:** A tiny sample of light-diffusing tissue is a later design option only; no guaranteed drop or crafting recipe is established.
- **Harvest impact:** Removing many adults or stripping occupied bark reduces local population and may expose juveniles to drying.
- **Renewal:** Depends on humidity, fungal food, retained deadwood, and reproductive survival.

## 8. Absorption and compatibility

A small target may be absorbable by an appropriately positioned early Slime, but access to a narrow crevice and the animal's retreat response make capture uncertain.

Potential learning branches:
- **Low-Light Contrast Adaptation:** Improve separation of bright/dark cues, not full night vision by default.
- **Surface-Grip Insight:** A possible later traction trait, subject to compatibility and movement-cost review.
- **Signal Pattern Recognition:** Better interpret short light pulses as local activity cues.

Absorption does not automatically grant bioluminescence, climbing immunity, or insect anatomy. Traits require compatibility evaluation and mastery.

## 9. Skills and training

The Bark Lantern is not a trainer. Observation may contribute to:
- **Crevice Search:** Systematically inspect shelter-bearing surfaces.
- **Low-Light Cue Reading:** Interpret faint, angle-dependent signals.
- **Deadwood Ecology:** Learn that habitat structures can be resources, not merely obstacles.

These are candidates; prerequisites, costs, practice stages, mastery and counterplay need formal skill records.

## 10. Variants and discovery

Provisional regional forms:
- **Rainbark Lantern:** More active during humid periods and on mossy bark.
- **Elderwood Lantern:** More likely to occupy large, old trees and deep hollows.

These are regional forms of one species, not separate species. Discovery clues include dim pulses, occupied crevices, fungal films, and repeated activity at dusk.
